How it works · Training a model from zero

A language model
built from zero

Joshua can build a small language model on your own computer, starting from random numbers: no existing model, no cloud. It is the same recipe as the big ones, at a size a laptop can handle. This page goes through every step, with the real numbers of Mattapedia, a 10-million-parameter model trained on Italian Wikipedia, taught to answer questions and then to speak like the speeches of the Italian President Sergio Mattarella.

The six steps

In Joshua, open 🧬 Train a new model in the menu on the left. The page has six steps, done in order; the fourth and fifth are optional.

Everything runs on the computer, with Apple's MLX library on the Mac's graphics chip. The times on this page were measured on a MacBook Air M4 with 16 GB of memory.

Download

A model learns everything it knows from the texts it reads, so the first step is getting them. Joshua offers two sources.

  • Wikipedia. The wikimedia/wikipedia collection on Hugging Face has every Wikipedia as plain text, one row per article, in files of a few hundred megabytes each (in the Parquet format). Joshua downloads them one at a time and counts the words as they arrive, stopping when it has as many as you asked for. The whole Italian Wikipedia is about 470 million words, 1.8 GB.
  • A folder. Every text file inside it and its subfolders: .txt, .md, .pdf, .epub, .docx, .odt, .html and .rtf. Nothing is downloaded; they are read where they are.

The Wikipedia files go into Hugging Face's own download folder, ~/.cache/huggingface/hub, where they stay: doing the step again is instant. Nothing is read yet: the step only lists what it got, in download.json.

Tokenization

A model doesn't read letters or words: it reads numbers. Tokenization decides how text is cut into pieces, called tokens, and gives each piece a number. It happens in three phases.

  1. Extracting the text. Every article or file becomes plain paragraphs in one temporary file (from a folder, repeated paragraphs are dropped), and a special marker, <|end|>, separates one document from the next.
  2. Learning the vocabulary. Joshua starts from the 256 possible bytes, so any character of any language can be written, then repeatedly joins the pair of pieces that appears most often into a new piece. This is byte-pair encoding (BPE), the method of GPT-2 and of most models since. It stops at 8,192 pieces, learned from the first 40 million words, which are plenty to choose them.
  3. Turning the texts into numbers. Every paragraph is cut with that vocabulary and written as numbers. One paragraph in 50 is put aside, never studied, to check the model on text it hasn't seen.

How the vocabulary is learned

The second phase is the heart of tokenization. It starts from the smallest pieces there are, the 256 possible bytes. In European languages these are mostly single letters, digits and punctuation; an accented letter like é takes two bytes, a Chinese character three. Then Joshua counts every pair of pieces that stand side by side anywhere in the texts, joins the most frequent pair into a new piece, and counts again with the new piece in place. Each round adds one piece. After 7,935 rounds the vocabulary is full: 8,192 pieces, that is the 256 bytes, the end marker and 7,935 new pieces.

Since the most frequent pair always goes first, the first rounds show what each language uses most. These are the first ten pieces learned from each Wikipedia; ␣ is a space, which sticks to the piece after it, and no piece ever joins across a space:

A frequent word ends up whole: Costituzione at round 6,984, Constitution at 5,382 in English, Verfassung at 3,662 in German. A rarer one stays in parts, like Konstytu|cja in Polish. Chinese has no spaces, so whole stretches of text are counted together and pieces can join several words: after rebuilding the characters, it joins 人民 ("people"), then 共和 ("republic"), until round 1,526 makes 中华人民共和国, "People's Republic of China", a single piece.

Common words become a single piece, rarer ones are built from parts. These are real cuts from a vocabulary of 8,192 pieces learned the same way from the first part of the English Wikipedia (the space belongs to the piece that follows it):

For Mattapedia, 150 million words became 268 million tokens to learn from, and 5.5 million kept aside. Each token is stored in two bytes, since 8,192 fits in the 65,536 values two bytes can hold: the whole of it is a 536 MB file, train.bin.

Sizing

Three numbers have to fit together: the size of the model, the size of the vocabulary and the amount of text.

The model

A model's size is its number of parameters: the numbers adjusted during training. Joshua offers three sizes.

SizeWidthLayersHeadsMiddle widthParameters
10M256841,02410.5 million
25M3841061,28023.8 million
50M5121281,79249.8 million

For comparison, the smallest models people chat with have a few billion parameters: a hundred to a thousand times more.

The vocabulary

Each piece of the vocabulary gets its own list of numbers, as long as the model is wide. With 8,192 pieces and a width of 256, that is 2.1 million parameters, a fifth of the 10M model. Llama 3's vocabulary of 128,000 pieces would take 33 million on its own, three times the whole model. A small model needs a small vocabulary: words get cut into more pieces, but the parameters go into understanding instead.

The text

A rule of thumb from DeepMind's research on training (the "Chinchilla" paper, 2022): about 20 tokens for every parameter. Fewer, and the model is starved; more, and a bigger model would do better with the same computing time. Joshua also stops at 4 passes over your texts, after which a model starts learning them by heart.

A step is one round of learning: 8 windows of 512 tokens, 4,096 tokens in all. The words are an estimate: with this vocabulary, Italian takes about 1.8 tokens a word.

The Llama structure

The model has the same structure as Meta's Llama models, and as most open models today: a decoder-only transformer, which reads tokens in order and predicts the next one. Each token can look only at the ones before it. That is what lets it write: predict a token, add it, predict the next.

  1. Embedding. Each token becomes a list of 256 numbers (the width), taken from a table with one row per piece of the vocabulary. At first these are random; training moves them, until pieces used in similar ways end up with similar numbers.
  2. Layers. The lists go through 8 identical layers, each of two parts, each with a shortcut that adds its result to what came in (a residual connection).
    • Attention: each token looks back at the previous ones and takes in what it needs from them. It does so with 4 heads at once, each free to look for something different, say grammar in one and the subject in another. Where a token is in the sentence is given by rotating its numbers by an angle that grows with the position (RoPE).
    • Feed-forward: each token on its own goes through a wider layer of 1,024 numbers (the middle width) and back to 256, with a gate that decides what passes (SwiGLU). Much of what the model knows is stored here.
    Before each part, the numbers are brought to a common scale (RMSNorm).
  3. Output. The last list is compared with every row of the embedding table, the same table as at the start, giving a score to each of the 8,192 pieces: the most likely next token scores highest.

The model sees 512 tokens at once, about 280 words. The whole configuration is a short file, the same one Hugging Face's Llama models use:

model/config.json · 10M
{"architectures": ["LlamaForCausalLM"], "model_type": "llama",

 "intermediate_size": 1024, "vocab_size": 8192, "max_position_embeddings": 512,

Pre-training

Training is one exercise repeated tens of thousands of times: take 8 windows of 512 tokens from random places in the texts, have the model guess every next token, measure how wrong it was, and nudge every parameter in the direction that would have made it a little less wrong.

  • How wrong is the loss (cross-entropy): 9.0 means guessing at random among 8,192 pieces; lower is better. Joshua draws it as a chart, measured on the texts it studies and on the paragraphs kept aside. When the two lines part, the model is learning by heart.
  • The nudge is done by the AdamW optimizer. Its size, the learning rate, grows over the first 500 steps to 0.001 (0.0008 for 25M, 0.0006 for 50M), then shrinks along a curve to a tenth of that by the end.
  • Every so often the model writes a sample, so you can watch it go from random pieces to words, to sentences, to something that reads like Wikipedia.

Mattapedia went from a loss of 5.06 at step 1,000 to 3.54 at the end. Its last sample, at step 51,222, is the shape of a Wikipedia list, with invented names and dates:

Questions and answers

A pre-trained model only continues text. Ask it "What is a car?" and it may go on with another question, as if it were reading a list. Chat models are taught to answer with a second round of training on questions paired with answers. Joshua makes them from Wikipedia itself: the first paragraph of an article is a definition, so it is a ready answer.

  • The question is made from the title: "Chi era …?" for people of the past (dates in brackets and "è stato"), "Chi è …?" for living ones, and otherwise one of "Che cos'è …?", "Cos'è …?", "Che cosa è …?", "Mi spieghi che cos'è …?", with the subject as the article names it ("l'automobile", not "Automobile").
  • The answer is the first paragraph, up to about 120 words. Disambiguation pages and lists are skipped.
  • Every pair is laid out the same way, after the end marker:
one training example · translated from Italian
<|end|>
Question: What is the car?
Answer: The car is a motor vehicle …

Training starts from the pre-trained model, with a learning rate three times smaller, so it bends rather than starts over. A share of each batch still comes from Wikipedia (25% for Mattapedia), so it doesn't forget it. 50,000 questions, about 2 million words, took 2,852 steps, about 12 minutes. The loss on questions it never saw went from 2.90 to 2.61; on Wikipedia it rose from 3.54 to 3.64, a little forgetting. A sample from the end:

sample · step 2,852 · translated from Italian
Question: What is Brusea?
Answer: Brusea (in Latin Bruxalis) is a fictional character of the Star Wars
science-fiction universe, created by J. K. Rowling.

The format is learned: restate the subject, then define it. The facts are invented; Star Wars was not created by J. K. Rowling.

A register of its own

The last training is on a folder of texts with a recognisable voice: an author, a newspaper, an institution. For Mattapedia, the 944 speeches of the Italian President Sergio Mattarella from 2015 to 2026, about a million words, collected in a public dataset on Kaggle. It works like a painting redone in another painter's style: the earlier steps give the model its language and what it knows; this one gives it a voice.

  • It starts from the last copy of step 4, with the same vocabulary and the same smaller learning rate, for 3 passes over the new texts.
  • A share of each batch (20% for Mattapedia) still comes from the questions and answers, so it keeps answering.
  • Too little training and it still sounds like before; too much and it forgets what it knew. This is called catastrophic forgetting. So Joshua keeps 8 copies along the way, and a third line on the chart shows how well the model still knows the previous step's texts.

For Mattapedia, the loss on speeches it never saw went from 3.43 to 3.16, while the one on questions and answers rose from 2.61 to 2.84: it took on the new voice, at some cost to the old skill.

Into Ollama

The trained model is saved in Hugging Face's layout: its weights (model.safetensors), config.json and tokenizer.json. Ollama, which runs the models in Joshua, wants a single file in the GGUF format. Adding a version to Joshua takes two steps.

  1. Conversion. llama.cpp's converter (convert_hf_to_gguf.py) packs the weights, the vocabulary and the settings into one file, at 16-bit precision: 21 MB for the 10M model. The converter recognises known vocabularies by a fingerprint, and ours is new; since it cuts text like GPT-2's, Joshua tells it to treat it as such.
  2. A Modelfile. Ollama needs a few settings: the template that wraps what you type, the token that ends an answer, how many tokens it sees at once, and how freely it picks the next one (the temperature). A model that went through step 4 gets the question-and-answer frame, so you type only the question.
Modelfile
FROM ./mattapedia.gguf
TEMPLATE """<|end|>
Domanda: {{ .Prompt }}
Risposta:"""
PARAMETER num_ctx 512
PARAMETER stop "<|end|>"
PARAMETER temperature 0.8

Then ollama create adds it, and it appears in Joshua's model menu. In the chat it gets only your words: no web search, no tools and no memory. It also works from a terminal, and in any program that uses Ollama:

terminal
ollama run Mattapedia "Che cos'è la Costituzione?"

What to expect

A model of 10 million parameters learns the form of a language very well: grammar, the shape of a definition, the tone of a speech. It cannot hold facts: for that it would need hundreds of times more parameters, and of text. Asked what the American Civil War was, Wikipedia-Italian, step 4's model, answered:

Wikipedia-Italian · translated from Italian
Question: What is the War of Secession?
Answer: The War of Secession is an international treaty concluded on 5 May 1917
between the French Republic, the government and the French Republic, which
took place on 28 May 1917.

Fluent, confident and wrong: the clearest way to see that a language model learns how things are said before it learns what is true. The big models make the same kind of mistake, only more rarely and more convincingly.
